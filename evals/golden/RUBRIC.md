# Golden-Set Labelling Rubric — ai-act-triage

## Preamble

- **What this is.** The decision rules used to assign `expected_tier` and `basis` in `cases.json`. Labels are the author's reading of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the EU AI Act) as a **non-lawyer**; this is a triage ground-truth for an engineering eval, **not legal advice**.
- **Corpus version.** Labels were verified against the consolidated text of Articles 5, 6 and 50 and Annex III as mirrored at artificialintelligenceact.eu (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, OJ L, 12.7.2024), fetched 2026-06-10. If the Commission's Article 6 guidelines or amendments change any operative text, re-audit contested labels first.
- **Core vs contested.** `core` cases fall near-deterministically out of the operative text. `contested` cases are genuinely arguable; their labels are decided by the numbered rules below, and each case's `rubric_note` names the deciding rule. An eval failure on a contested case is a signal to read the rationale, not automatically a model bug.
- **Basis convention.** For `prohibited`, `high_risk`, and `limited` cases, `basis` lists the provisions that positively determine the label. For `minimal` cases, `basis` lists the **nearest-miss provisions considered and found inapplicable** (the temptation the rationale defuses) — a minimal label is determined by absence, so the citations document what was ruled out.
- **Tier ordering.** prohibited > high_risk > limited > minimal. One tier per case; overlapping obligations (e.g. Article 50 transparency on a high-risk system) are noted in rationale only.

## Rules

**R1 — Profiling defeats the derogations.** If a system performs profiling of natural persons within an Annex III use case, the Article 6(3) escape hatches are textually unavailable ("shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons") → `high_risk`. Scoring, ranking, or characterising individual people is profiling. *Basis: Article 6(3), final subparagraph.*

**R2 — Narrow-procedural / preparatory escape (and its converse).** Inside an Annex III area, a system that only transforms or organises information — field extraction, OCR, summarisation with citations, redaction, formatting — without evaluating any personal aspect or producing a person-level score, flag, or ordering, escapes via Article 6(3)(a) (narrow procedural task) or 6(3)(d) (preparatory task) → `minimal`, with the provider's documentation/registration duties noted in rationale. **Converse:** a per-person output that triggers consequences (misconduct proceedings, queue position, disconnection priority) materially influences the assessment and does **not** escape. *Basis: Article 6(3)(a), Article 6(3)(d).*

**R3 — Strictest applicable function sets the tier.** If any function of the system as marketed falls under a stricter tier, the whole system takes that tier (e.g. a call-analytics suite whose agent-facing emotion scoring is an Article 5(1)(f) practice is `prohibited`, even though its customer-facing half alone would be `high_risk`). *Basis: tier ordering; Article 5 practices cannot be cured by bundling.*

**R4 — Internal-context chatbots → minimal.** Article 50(1) requires disclosure "unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect." Where users are employees informed by onboarding/policy and the interface is explicitly badged as AI, the duty is discharged by design and context; no operative obligation remains, so triage tier = `minimal`, not `limited`. Public-facing or unbadged conversational systems stay `limited`. *Basis: Article 50(1).*

**R5 — Emotion recognition requires biometric inference of emotions.** Under Article 3(39), an emotion recognition system identifies or infers **emotions or intentions** from **biometric data**. Physical states (fatigue, pain — Recital 18 names driver-fatigue detection) and text-only sentiment analysis are outside the definition, hence outside both the Article 5(1)(f) ban and Annex III point 1(c) / Article 50(3). *Basis: Article 3(39); Recital 18.*

**R6 — The listed use-case text governs, in both directions.** Operating in a sensitive sector (police, border, education, energy) does not raise the tier unless the system matches a listed use case; explicit exclusions are taken at face value (Annex III point 1(a) identity verification, point 5(b) financial-fraud detection, point 7(d) travel-document authenticity, point 8(b) campaign logistics) → `minimal`. Symmetrically, relabelling cannot lower the tier: a system whose function matches a listed use case takes that classification whatever it is called ("collections optimisation" that predicts a person's likelihood to pay is creditworthiness evaluation). *Basis: Article 6(2), Annex III operative text.*

**R7 — Social scoring has two limbs.** The Article 5(1)(c) practice requires (i) evaluation/classification based on social behaviour or personal characteristics **and** (ii) detrimental or unfavourable treatment either in social contexts unrelated to where the data was generated, or unjustified/disproportionate to the behaviour. Perk differentiation confined to the same commercial relationship meets neither limb → `minimal`. Cross-context scores gating housing, services, or opportunities → `prohibited` (applies to private actors too). *Basis: Article 5(1)(c).*

**R8 — GPAI is an overlay, never a tier.** General-purpose model obligations (Articles 51-56), including systemic-risk duties, attach to the model provider and do not change the use-case classification. Classify the deployed use case; mention GPAI duties in rationale. *Basis: Articles 51-56 by structure; Article 6 and Annex III tier uses, not models.*

**R9 — Component vs product: intended purpose as placed on the market decides.** A general-purpose component (OCR, transcription, embeddings API) marketed without a high-risk intended purpose is classified on its own intended purpose, not its customer's integration; the party that integrates it into an Annex III system becomes the provider of that high-risk system. *Basis: Article 6 ("intended to be used"); Article 25 (value-chain responsibilities).*

**R10 — The Article 6(1) route is functional and mechanical.** If the AI is a safety component of (or is itself) a product covered by Annex I Section A harmonisation legislation (machinery, lifts, medical devices, in-vitro diagnostics…) **and** that product requires third-party conformity assessment → `high_risk`, regardless of how mundane the function sounds, and the Article 6(3) derogations (which apply only to Annex III systems) cannot be invoked. Analytics that do not perform the safety function (maintenance scheduling, wear prediction) are not safety components. Wellness/information products that do not trigger third-party assessment under the sectoral law do not enter via 6(1). *Basis: Article 6(1).*

**R11 — The Article 5(1)(f) medical/safety exception is narrow, then check downstream.** Workplace or education emotion inference deployed strictly for medical or safety reasons escapes the ban — but the system must then still be checked against Annex III point 1(c) (if it is emotion recognition under R5) and point 4(b) (if outputs reach management decisions). A system that escapes under R5's definition (physical state, not emotion) and feeds no employment decision lands at `minimal`. *Basis: Article 5(1)(f); Annex III points 1(c), 4(b).*

**R12 — Article 50 assigns `limited` only when nothing higher applies.** Chatbot disclosure (50(1)), synthetic-content marking (50(2)), emotion/biometric-categorisation information duties (50(3)), and deepfake/AI-text labelling (50(4)) place a system at `limited` only if no Article 5 practice and no high-risk route is engaged; otherwise the transparency duty is noted in rationale on top of the higher tier. *Basis: Article 50(1)-(4).*
